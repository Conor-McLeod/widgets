Large language models are now routinely stored, and increasingly trained, with 4-bit numbers. That
sounds impossible: four bits is sixteen codes. Both formats in use, **MXFP4** and **NVFP4**, make it work
the same way. They store tiny floats and give every small block of them a shared scale factor. They
differ in how big the block is and how precise its scale is, and that difference turns out to
matter.

## Four bits of float

The element type in both is FP4 **E2M1**: one sign bit, two exponent bits and one mantissa bit. Like
any float (the [float64 exploration](/float64) has the full story), a normal value is

<M block t="(-1)^{s} \times 2^{\,e - 1} \times 1.m_2" />

with exponent bias 1, and exponent field 0 is a subnormal, <M t="0.m_2" />. There are no infinities and
no NaN. Click through all sixteen codes.

<Widget src="e2m1" height={250} />

That leaves <M t="0, \pm 0.5, \pm 1, \pm 1.5, \pm 2, \pm 3, \pm 4, \pm 6" />. It's fifteen values, since
<M t="+0" /> and <M t="-0" /> are the same number. Notice the spacing: half-steps near zero, then
steps of 1, then 2. Relative precision is roughly constant, which suits weights and activations whose
sizes spread over orders of magnitude.

On its own this range is useless. The values in a weight matrix might all sit around <M t="0.01" />, and
an activation might hit 300. The fix is a **scale**: store <M t="x / s" /> in FP4 and keep <M t="s" />
somewhere else, so the value is <M t="s \times \text{fp4}" />. One scale for a whole tensor isn't
enough, because a single large value would force a large <M t="s" /> and everything small would round
to zero. So the tensor is cut into short **blocks**, each with its own scale, chosen so that the block's
largest magnitude maps near the top of the FP4 range, 6.

## Two ways to pick a scale

**MXFP4** comes from the Open Compute Project's Microscaling (MX) spec, written jointly by AMD, Arm,
Intel, Meta, Microsoft, NVIDIA and Qualcomm. A block is 32 values and its scale is **E8M0**: eight bits
of exponent and nothing else, so the scale is always a power of two, <M t="2^{k}" /> with
<M t="k" /> from <M t="-127" /> to <M t="127" />. The spec sets

<M block t="s = 2^{\,\lfloor \log_2 \mathit{amax} \rfloor - 2}" />

where <M t="\mathit{amax}" /> is the block's largest magnitude. The <M t="-2" /> is the exponent of E2M1's
largest binade, so <M t="\mathit{amax} / s" /> always lands in <M t="[4, 8)" />.

**NVFP4** is NVIDIA's format, introduced with its Blackwell GPUs. A block is 16 values, and its scale is
an 8-bit **E4M3** float, which has three mantissa bits. So the scale can be (nearly) any number, and
NVFP4 picks <M t="s \approx \mathit{amax} / 6" />.

Slide the block's largest value and watch where it lands on each format's grid. The blue ticks are the
FP4 values times the scale; the shaded band is beyond <M t="6s" />, where nothing can be stored.

<Widget src="scale" height={330} caption="The other values in the block would be stored on the same grid, rounded to the nearest tick." />

A power-of-two scale can only move the grid in octaves. When <M t="\mathit{amax} / s" /> falls in
<M t="(6, 8)" />, the largest value is past the top tick and **clips** to 6, losing up to 25%. That's the
case at the slider's starting point, about 7. When it falls just above 4, the value 6 sits unused above the block's
maximum, and the block effectively loses the top of its grid. NVFP4's scale puts <M t="\mathit{amax}" />
within a few percent of 6 every time, so the whole grid is put to use. (The scale is itself rounded to
three mantissa bits, so the maximum can land a little above 6 and clip by a few percent, as it does at
the start.)

### Why NVFP4 needs a second scale

E8M0 spans 254 octaves, so an MXFP4 scale can be anything a tensor needs. E4M3 only reaches from
<M t="2^{-9}" /> to 448, about 17 octaves, and its precision is what makes it useful. NVFP4 therefore adds
one FP32 scale per tensor, <M t="\mathit{amax}_{\text{tensor}} / (6 \times 448)" />, and each block's
E4M3 scale is stored relative to it. Dequantizing an element is

<M block t="x \approx s_{\text{tensor}} \times s_{\text{block}} \times \text{fp4}" />

That one FP32 per tensor is negligible. The per-block scales are not: an 8-bit scale per 32 values costs
MXFP4 <M t="8/32 = 0.25" /> extra bits per value, and one per 16 costs NVFP4 0.5. NVFP4 spends a quarter-bit
more per value on finer blocks and finer scales.

## Outliers and block size

Small blocks matter most when a tensor has outliers, and the activations of large language models are
known for them: a few channels hold values far larger than the rest. One big value sets the scale for
its whole block, and the ordinary values beside it get crushed toward zero.

Below, 64 values from a normal distribution are quantized both ways, with one value replaced by 12.
Compare the outlier's block in each row, and toggle the outlier off to see the difference without it.

<Widget src="block" height={470} caption="The axis is cut off at the largest ordinary value; the triangle marks the outlier." />

With the outlier, MXFP4's block scale jumps to <M t="2^{1}" />. Its smallest step is then <M t="0.5 \times 2 = 1" />,
so anything under 0.5 rounds to zero, across all 32 values of the block. NVFP4 confines the
damage to 16 values, and its scale is <M t="12/6 = 2" /> as well, but only in that one block. The other
three blocks keep their own, smaller scales. Without the outlier the gap narrows, but NVFP4 still wins,
mostly from fitting <M t="\mathit{amax}" /> to 6 instead of to a power of two.

## Where they're used

Both are hardware formats. NVIDIA's Blackwell [Tensor Cores](/tensor-core) multiply FP4 blocks directly,
applying the block scales inside the matrix instruction and accumulating in FP32, and they accept both
MXFP4 and NVFP4. AMD's MI350 series supports the MX formats. OpenAI released the gpt-oss weights in
MXFP4, and NVIDIA has published results on pretraining language models in NVFP4.

Neither format is a drop-in replacement for higher precision. Practical recipes keep sensitive layers
in higher precision, use stochastic rounding for gradients, and apply random rotations (Hadamard
transforms) that spread outliers across a block before quantizing. The block scale is the part both
formats share, and it is what makes four bits workable in the first place.
