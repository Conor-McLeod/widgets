Say it's noon, a goal has a 17:00 deadline, and you owe it an hour of work. You have five hours, and one of
them has to be work, so you need to use 20% of your remaining time on it. That's the **required utilisation**:

$$
r = \frac{L}{T}
$$

where $L$ is the work left and $T$ is the time left until the deadline. Do nothing and $T$ shrinks while
$L$ stays put. At 13:00 it's 25%, at 14:00 33%, at 15:00 50%, and at 16:00 100%.

The point of tracking it is a fact about people rather than physics. Five hours of work in five hours is
*possible*, but nobody keeps up 100%. Something like 70% is a more honest ceiling once you count switching,
breaks and the things that come up. Call that ceiling $\theta$. As long as $r \le \theta$ you can still finish at
a pace you can actually keep. Once $r > \theta$ you're relying on everything going to plan.

So $r$ is a sensible thing to watch. But it has a nasty habit. Try the first schedule below, which is the
one that prompted this page: do 50 of the 60 minutes straight away, then leave the last 10 minutes "for later".

Things to try:

- **Most of it, then coast.** Required utilisation drops to single figures and stays low for hours. It looks completely safe.
  Then it shoots up near the end: it passes 70% with about 15 minutes to go. The slack line underneath has been
  falling steadily the whole time, and it hits zero at exactly that same moment. Nothing about the slack is a surprise.
- **Wait, then flat out.** This is the plan your brain proposes ("I'll start at four"). It needs 100% from the
  moment it starts, and the slack ran out at about 15:34, well before you begin.
- **Little and often.** A steady trickle holds $r$ level at its starting 20%. Notice that the slack still falls.
- **Make the window 12 hours** and the work due 2 hours. Every schedule now starts with lots of slack, and
  you can see how much coasting that slack can absorb.

## Why it swings: the $1/T$ in the denominator

Suppose you spend a fraction $u$ of each passing hour on the goal. That's your actual, or **current**,
utilisation. The work left falls at rate $u$ and the time left falls at rate 1:

$$
\frac{dL}{dt} = -u, \qquad \frac{dT}{dt} = -1
$$

Apply the quotient rule to $r = L/T$:

$$
\frac{dr}{dt} = \frac{-uT + L}{T^2} = \frac{r - u}{T}
$$

Two things come out of this.

1. **The same $r$ moves at very different speeds.** When you're idle ($u = 0$), $r$ climbs at $r/T$ per
   hour. At 16.7% with ten hours to go that's 1.7 points an hour. At 16.7% with one hour to go it's
   16.7 points an hour. The number on the dial is the same, but the danger isn't.
2. **What matters is how your current utilisation compares with $r$.** If $u > r$, $r$ falls. If $u < r$, it
   rises. Showing current utilisation next to required utilisation tells you which way you're heading, which
   the required figure alone can't.

10 hours away. Then you stop working on all three.

With $u = 0$ the work left is fixed, so $r$ traces a hyperbola, $r(t) = L / (T_0 - t)$, which runs off to
infinity at the deadline. The three curves start at the same height, but the 1-hour goal runs out of slack ten times
sooner than the 10-hour one. A ratio throws away scale, and scale is exactly what decides how much trouble you're in.

## Holding steady is balancing on a knife-edge

Integrate $dr/dt$ at a constant pace $u$ and the solution has a neat closed form:

$$
r(T) = u + (r_0 - u)\,\frac{T_0}{T}
$$

The gap between $r$ and $u$ grows like $1/T$ as the deadline gets closer. The level line $r = u$ is an
**unstable** equilibrium. Start just below your pace and you finish early. Start just above it and the gap
compounds until it's beyond 100%. No start drifts back to the line on its own.

## Slack: the same information, in hours

The slack is how long you could stop working before the pace you'd need passes $\theta$:

$$
S = T - \frac{L}{\theta} = T\left(1 - \frac{r}{\theta}\right)
$$

It holds exactly what $r$ holds, multiplied by $T$, so the scale is back. And its dynamics are dull, which is what you want from a gauge:

$$
\frac{dS}{dt} = \frac{u}{\theta} - 1
$$

There's no $T$ in that, so nothing speeds up as the deadline closes in.

- **When you're idle you lose exactly one hour of slack per hour.** That's true at noon and at 16:50 alike,
  so the danger the first widget shows can't sneak up on you.
- **Slack is in the units disruptions come in.** Dinner takes an hour and a half, not 12%. "I have 2h40
  of slack" answers the real question directly: how big a surprise can I absorb?
- **Working below $\theta$ still burns slack.** It only grows while you work faster than $\theta$. Even at 100% you
  earn just $1/\theta - 1 \approx 0.43$ hours per hour. The way to build slack is the dull one: start early.

$r$ and $S$ always agree at the boundary ($S = 0$ exactly when $r = \theta$). Neither is wrong. $r$ is a decent gauge of how hard you have to work from now on. $S$ is the honest gauge of how safe you are.

## What this leaves out

- **Not every hour is available.** $T$ above is wall-clock time, so it counts sleep, meals and appointments.
  At 22:00, with 3 hours due by 17:00 tomorrow, $r = 3/19 \approx 16\%$. Take out 8 hours of sleep and it's
  $3/11 \approx 27\%$. The ceiling $\theta$ only makes sense against hours you could actually work.
- **Disruptions grow with $T$.** If a fifth of your waking time tends to get taken by things you didn't plan
  for, fold that into the ceiling: $\theta \approx 0.7 \times 0.8 = 0.56$.
- **Several goals share the same hours.** Working towards a later deadline also has to cover the earlier ones, so
  each deadline's $L$ is everything due by then. You can finish everything at pace $\theta$ exactly when
  every $S \ge 0$, which is the same as every $r \le \theta$. But to pick which deadline is *most* at risk, rank
  by the smallest $S$, not the largest $r$.
