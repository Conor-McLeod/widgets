The original Transformer put a **LayerNorm** after every sublayer. Most recent large models (LLaMA, Mistral,
Gemma and friends) use **RMSNorm** instead. It is LayerNorm with a step taken out, which raises the obvious
question: what did that step do, and why is nobody missing it?

For a vector *x* with *d* entries, LayerNorm centres it and then rescales it:

> LayerNorm(x)<sub>i</sub> = (x<sub>i</sub> − μ) / σ · γ<sub>i</sub> + β<sub>i</sub>

RMSNorm ([Zhang & Sennrich, 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.07467)) skips the centring and the bias.
It only divides by the root-mean-square:

> RMS(x) = √( (1/d) Σ x<sub>i</sub><sup>2</sup> + ε ),  RMSNorm(x)<sub>i</sub> = x<sub>i</sub> / RMS(x) · γ<sub>i</sub>

The widgets below leave γ at 1, since a learned per-channel gain just stretches the result afterwards.

## Scale goes, shape stays

Drag the bars to edit an 8-dimensional input. The dashed blue lines mark ±RMS(x). Dividing by RMS(x) moves
those lines to ±1, so the output always has an RMS of exactly 1. Things to try:

- Press **× 2** or **÷ 2** a few times. The input grows and shrinks but the output doesn't change at all.
  RMSNorm only cares about the *direction* of x, not its length.
- Press **+ 1**. Now the output does change. RMSNorm is not invariant to a shift. Switch to
  **LayerNorm** and press **+ 1** again: subtracting the mean (dashed red line) cancels the shift.
- Try **all equal**. RMSNorm gives every entry 1. LayerNorm gives every entry 0, because once you subtract
  the mean nothing is left.
- Try **outlier**. One big entry inflates RMS(x) and squeezes everything else towards zero. Both norms do this.

So LayerNorm buys one extra property, invariance to adding a constant to every channel. The RMSNorm paper's
bet was that the rescaling was what helped training, and the re-centring wasn't. Experiments backed this up:
models trained just as well, and the norm got cheaper. It needs one reduction over the vector (Σx²) instead of
two (the mean, then the variance around it), and there's no β to store. A normalization runs twice per layer,
so that saving adds up.

## The geometry

With γ = 1, every RMSNorm output has Σy<sub>i</sub><sup>2</sup> = d. That means RMSNorm is just
*project x radially onto the sphere of radius √d*. LayerNorm does two projections. First it subtracts
the mean, which drops x onto the hyperplane where the entries sum to zero. Then it scales onto the same sphere.
Its outputs live on the sphere *intersected with* that hyperplane, which has one dimension fewer.

You can see this most clearly with d = 2, where the plane is easy to draw. Drag **x** around.

<Widget src="geometry" height={470} caption="A 2-D input. Blue: RMSNorm's output on the circle of radius √2. Red: LayerNorm's projection onto x₁ + x₂ = 0, then its output." />

In 2-D, RMSNorm can reach any point on the circle. LayerNorm can only output (1, −1) or (−1, 1). Centring
removed one of the two degrees of freedom, and scaling removed the other. In a real model *d* is in the
thousands, so losing one dimension is negligible. That's a good argument for why the centring step doesn't
matter much: it barely changes where outputs can go, and the next linear layer can learn any offset it needs.

Now turn up **ε** and drag x close to the origin. Without ε, a tiny input gets blown up onto the circle
just like a big one. With ε, the denominator never drops below √ε, so small inputs stay small and division
by zero is impossible. Real models use something like ε = 10<sup>−5</sup> or 10<sup>−6</sup>, which only matters
for vectors very close to zero.
