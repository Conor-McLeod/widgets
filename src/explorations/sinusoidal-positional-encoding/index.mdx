Self-attention has no idea what order its inputs are in. Every token scores every other token with a dot
product, then takes a weighted sum, and neither step looks at where the tokens sit. Shuffle the input and
each token's output stays the same; the outputs just come back shuffled. "Dog bites man" and "man bites dog"
look like the same bag of words.

The original Transformer ([Vaswani et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762)) fixed this by adding a
**positional encoding** to each token embedding before the first layer. It's a vector of
size $d_{\text{model}}$ that depends only on the position $p$ of a token in the input sequence. Nothing about it is learned. It's built from sines and cosines:

These formulas define the values of the $2i$-th and $(2i + 1)$-th dimensions of the positional embedding for token $p$ in the input sequence: 
$$
\operatorname{PE}(p)_{2i} = \sin(p\,\omega_i), \qquad \operatorname{PE}(p)_{2i+1} = \cos(p\,\omega_i), \qquad \omega_i = \frac{1}{10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}
$$

where:
$$
i = 0, 1, \dots, \frac{d_{\text{model}}}{2} - 1.   
$$

$i$ is the "dimension-pair index" --- it indexes every pair of consecutive dimensions in the positional embedding up until the final pair, $d_{\text{model}} - 2$ and $d_{\text{model}} - 1$. To be explicit:
- $i=0$ covers dimensions $2i = 0$ and $2i + 1 = 1$
- $i=1$ covers dimensions $2i = 2$ and $2i + 1 = 3$
- $i=\frac{d_{\text{model}}}{2} - 1$ covers dimensions $2i = d_{\text{model}} - 2$ and $2i + 1 = d_{\text{model}} - 1$

$d_{\text{model}}$ is assumed to always be even so that there is never a $\operatorname{PE}(p)_{2i}$ without a $\operatorname{PE}(p)_{2i+1}$.

That looks arbitrary at first. Why sines, why pairs, and why 10000? The widgets below answer these one at a time.

## A bank of clocks

Take the dimensions two at a time. Pair $i$ holds $\sin p\,\omega_i$ and $\cos p\,\omega_i$. Write them cosine first,
$(\cos p\,\omega_i,\ \sin p\,\omega_i)$, and that's a point on the unit circle at angle $p\,\omega_i$, measured
anticlockwise from the $x$-axis as usual. The paper puts the sine first, but which coordinate you list first is just
a labelling choice and changes nothing below. So you can think of each pair as a clock hand, one that starts at
3 o'clock and turns anticlockwise. Every step forward in position turns hand $i$ by $\omega_i$ radians, and it comes
back round every $\lambda_i = 2\pi / \omega_i$ positions.

The frequencies fall off geometrically, so pair 0 spins once every 6.3 positions and the last pair takes
thousands of positions to make one turn. Below is a tiny $d_{\text{model}} = 16$ encoding, with 8 hands.
The numbers under each dial are the hand's coordinates, cosine first: the two entries of the vector that the hand stands for.

- Press **play** and watch the hands. It works like an odometer, or the bits of a binary counter. The fast
  hands tell nearby positions apart, and the slow hands tell you roughly where you are. No two positions put all eight hands in the same place.
- The fast hands wrap round often, so on their own they're ambiguous: positions 0 and 44 look almost the same on hand 0.
  The slower hands break the tie.

### Shifting is a rotation

Now set the **offset** $k$ to something like 5. The dashed hands show position $p + k$, and the arc
shows how far each hand turned to get there. Then drag $p$ around. **The arcs don't change.** Going $k$ steps
forward turns hand $i$ by $k\,\omega_i$, wherever you start.

That's the property the paper was after. For each pair, moving by $k$ is a fixed rotation:

$$
\begin{pmatrix} \cos (p+k)\omega_i \\ \sin (p+k)\omega_i \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos k\omega_i & -\sin k\omega_i \\ \sin k\omega_i & \cos k\omega_i \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \cos p\,\omega_i \\ \sin p\,\omega_i \end{pmatrix}
$$

The matrix depends on $k$ but not on $p$. Stack those 2×2 blocks down the diagonal, and
$\operatorname{PE}(p+k)$ is a single linear map of $\operatorname{PE}(p)$. The authors guessed this would make
it easy for the model to learn "look 3 tokens back", because a linear layer can implement that shift in exactly the same way at every position.
This is why the encoding uses sin *and* cos. A sine on its own can't be rotated by a linear map; you need both coordinates of the point.

## The whole matrix

Stack the encodings for every position side by side and you get the familiar striped picture. Each column below is one position's
vector and each row is one dimension. The map shows all the sine rows first and then all the cosine rows, as many implementations
store them. The order doesn't matter to the model, since the next layer is a linear map that can permute dimensions however it likes.
In each half, the top rows are the fast clocks, and they flicker. Further down the rows
are slower, until near the bottom they barely change across 128 positions: sines sit near 0 and cosines near 1. Hover to read off any entry and its wavelength.

Things to try:

- Tick **interleave** to see the paper's order, where each sine row sits next to its cosine. The slow end turns into
  alternating white and blue stripes: the same numbers, shuffled.

- Move $d_{\text{model}}$. The wavelengths always run from $2\pi$ to almost $2\pi \cdot 10000$, so a bigger
  model doesn't get faster or slower clocks, just more of them in between.
- Turn the **base** down to 10. The slowest wavelength is now about $2\pi \cdot 10 \approx 63$, so every row cycles within the 128
  positions on screen, and the bottom of the map stops being a smooth gradient. The base sets the longest stretch of positions the encoding can tell apart.
  10000 was the paper's choice for sequences up to a few thousand tokens, with lots of room to spare.

## Distance from dot products

Attention compares vectors with dot products, so it's worth asking what the dot product of two encodings looks like.
Each pair gives $\sin a \sin b + \cos a \cos b = \cos(a - b)$, so

$$
\operatorname{PE}(p) \cdot \operatorname{PE}(q) = \sum_{i} \cos\big((p - q)\,\omega_i\big)
$$

The positions only enter through $p - q$. The plot shows this sum (divided by $d_{\text{model}}/2$, so it peaks at 1)
for a fixed $p$ against every $q$. Drag on the plot, or use the slider, to move $p$. The curve slides along
without changing shape. It peaks at $q = p$, drops off for nearby positions, and is the same on both sides, because cosine is even:
on its own, the dot product can tell how far away a position is but not whether it's before or after.

The red circle marks the position at least 4 steps away that looks most like $p$.

- Set $d_{\text{model}} = 2$. With a single clock, the similarity is just $\cos(p - q)$. Position 84 is 44 steps
  from $p = 40$, almost exactly 7 turns, so it scores 1.00 and is indistinguishable from $p$ itself.
- Double $d_{\text{model}}$ a few times. Each new clock damps those false peaks. By 16 the red circle jumps to just outside the
  excluded zone: the most similar distant position is now simply the closest one, and similarity mostly falls with distance.
- The curve never drops to zero, though. Over 160 positions the slow clocks hardly turn, so each one adds about 1 to the sum,
  and the floor sits around 0.45. Attention cares about differences between scores, so a constant floor costs little.
- Now lower the **base**. At 100 the bump gets narrower and the floor drops to about 0. At 10 there's just a spike at $p$
  and noise everywhere else. Every position is now very distinct, but the dot product no longer tells a position 2 steps away
  from one 100 steps away. The base trades sharpness for a smooth sense of distance.

## What came after

The paper found that learned position embeddings worked about as well, and chose the sinusoids partly in the hope
that they would extend to sequences longer than any seen in training. That hope mostly didn't pan out. Adding position to the
embedding also mixes "what" and "where" into one vector before attention ever sees them.

The idea that lasted is the rotation. **RoPE** ([Su et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.09864)), used in LLaMA and most
open models since, takes the same bank of frequencies $\omega_i$ with the same base of 10000. But instead of adding the
encoding to the embedding, it *rotates* each pair of query and key coordinates by $p\,\omega_i$. Then the query–key dot
product depends on position only through $p - q$, by construction, in every layer. It's the clocks above, applied directly where
attention compares tokens.
