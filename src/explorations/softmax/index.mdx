A classifier's last layer produces one number per class: the **logits**. They can be any real numbers, negative or
positive, and they don't add up to anything in particular. What we usually want is a probability distribution: one
non-negative number per class, summing to 1, with the biggest logit getting the biggest share. We also want it to be
smooth, so that gradient descent can tell how nudging each logit would change the answer.

The obvious ideas each fail one of those. Dividing each logit by the sum breaks as soon as a logit is negative, or the
sum is 0. Picking the largest (argmax) gives a one-hot answer that doesn't budge when you nudge a logit, so its gradient
is zero almost everywhere. **Softmax** makes every score positive by exponentiating it, then normalises:

$$
\operatorname{softmax}(z)_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}}
$$

Every $e^{z_i}$ is positive, dividing by the total makes them sum to 1, and $e^x$ is increasing, so the order of the
logits is kept.

Things to try:

- Press **+ 1** a few times. Every logit goes up, and the probabilities don't move at all. Adding $c$ to every logit
  multiplies every $e^{z_i}$ by the same $e^{c}$, and that factor cancels against the sum. Only the *differences*
  between logits matter.
- That's because the ratio of two probabilities is $p_i / p_j = e^{z_i - z_j}$, shown under the table. A gap of 1 in
  the logits makes one class $e \approx 2.7$ times as likely as the other. A gap of $\ln 10 \approx 2.3$ makes it 10
  times as likely, and a gap of 4.6 makes it 100 times as likely. Logits are log-odds, up to that shared constant.
- Drag one logit well above the rest. Its probability heads towards 1 but never reaches it, and the losers keep a small
  share. That's the "soft" in softmax: it's a smoothed-out version of argmax, which would put all of the mass on the
  winner.
- Press **× 2**. The gaps double, so every ratio gets squared, and the distribution gets peakier. **÷ 2** flattens it.

## Temperature

Scaling all the logits is common enough to have a name. Dividing them by a **temperature** $T$ before the softmax,

$$
p_i = \frac{e^{z_i / T}}{\sum_j e^{z_j / T}}
$$

stretches every gap by $1/T$. Move the slider in the widget above.

- As $T \to 0$ the gaps blow up and softmax turns into argmax: the top class gets everything.
- At $T = 1$ you get plain softmax.
- As $T \to \infty$ the gaps shrink to nothing and every class gets $1/5$, the dashed line.

Language models sample their next token from a softmax over the vocabulary, and the "temperature" setting in an LLM API
is this $T$. Low temperatures make the model pick the likeliest token almost every time; high ones flatten the
distribution and let unlikely tokens through.

## Three classes, drawn

With three classes we can draw the whole map. Since adding a constant to every logit changes nothing, three logits only
have two degrees of freedom, so the left panel shows the logits as a plane. The three arrows point in the directions
that raise $z_A$, $z_B$ and $z_C$. Moving perpendicular to the page, adding the same amount to all three, would do nothing,
so there's no third axis.

The right panel shows the probabilities. Three non-negative numbers summing to 1 live in a triangle (the **probability
simplex**): each corner is one class with probability 1, the centre is $(\tfrac13, \tfrac13, \tfrac13)$, and the
closer the point is to a corner, the more likely that class. The grid on the left is pushed through softmax and drawn on
the right. The dashed lines are where two logits tie; they meet at the centre of each panel.

- Drag the point far out on the left. On the right it crowds into a corner or an edge, but never touches one, however
  far you go. Softmax maps the whole infinite plane into the open triangle.
- Look at how the grid deforms. Near the centre the squares are nearly undistorted; softmax is roughly linear for small
  logits. Far from the centre whole rows of squares get squashed into a sliver along an edge. Those are **saturated**
  logits: big changes on the left barely move the probabilities on the right, which is why gradients through a confident
  softmax get small.
- Lower $T$. The point (and the grid) races towards the edges, because the same logits now count for more. Raise it and
  everything collapses towards the centre.
- Drag on the right to see the inverse. Any point inside the triangle comes from a line of logit vectors, $z_i = T \ln p_i + c$
  for any $c$. The left panel shows the one with $c$ chosen so the logits sum to 0.

With only two classes the triangle becomes a line segment, and softmax becomes something familiar:

$$
p_1 = \frac{e^{z_1}}{e^{z_1} + e^{z_2}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(z_1 - z_2)}} = \sigma(z_1 - z_2)
$$

That's the logistic sigmoid of the logit difference. Softmax is the many-class version of logistic regression's sigmoid.

## Why it trains nicely

Softmax is almost always followed by the cross-entropy loss, $L = -\ln p_y$ for the correct class $y$. The derivative of
softmax itself is

$$
\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial z_j} = p_i\,(\delta_{ij} - p_j)
$$

which shrinks when the distribution saturates, as the grid above suggests. But chain it through the loss and the
logarithm undoes the exponential:

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_j} = p_j - [j = y]
$$

The gradient on each logit is just "what the model predicted minus what it should have predicted". If the model is
confidently wrong, with $p_y$ near 0, the correct class still gets a gradient near $-1$ pulling it up. The saturation
only bites when the model is already right.

## Overflow, and subtracting the max

The formula as written is a numerical trap. $e^{z}$ grows quickly: in float32 it overflows to infinity for any $z$ above
about 88.7, and in float16 above about 11. Logits of 100 aren't unusual in practice, and once two of the $e^{z_i}$ are
infinite the division is $\infty / \infty$, which is NaN. Very negative logits fail the other way: every $e^{z_i}$
underflows to 0, and the result is $0/0$.

The fix uses the shift invariance from earlier. Subtracting $\max_j z_j$ from every logit doesn't change the answer, and
afterwards the largest exponent is exactly $e^0 = 1$. Nothing can overflow, the sum is at least 1 so it can't be 0, and
the only things that underflow are probabilities too small to matter. Every real softmax implementation does this.

The widget runs both versions with every operation rounded to the chosen format. Slide the shift $c$, which adds the
same constant to all three logits, so the right answer never changes.

<Widget src="stability" height={420} />

- In float32, push $c$ past about 86. The naive version's largest $e^{z_i}$ becomes $\infty$ and its outputs turn into
  NaN and 0. The right-hand column doesn't change.
- Switch to float16. Now $c = 9$ is already enough. This is one reason mixed-precision training keeps the softmax in
  float32, and why attention kernels are careful about where they do it.
- Go negative. The naive sum underflows to 0, and $0/0$ is NaN again.
- Try $c = 1000$ in float16. The stable version still gives finite answers, but they're slightly off: float16 values
  near 1000 are spaced 0.5 apart, so the logit $1000.2$ is stored as $1000$ before softmax ever sees it. Subtracting
  the max saves you from overflow, not from the precision you lost upstream.

Libraries also offer a fused **log-softmax**, $z_i - \max z - \ln \sum_j e^{z_j - \max z}$, because taking $\ln$ of a
probability that has already underflowed to 0 gives $-\infty$. Computing it from the logits directly avoids that.

## In attention

Softmax isn't only for classifiers. Every attention layer in a transformer turns query–key scores into weights with it:
$\operatorname{softmax}(q \cdot k_j / \sqrt{d})$ over the keys $k_j$. The $\sqrt{d}$ is a temperature. Dot products of
$d$-dimensional vectors grow like $\sqrt{d}$, and without the division the softmax would saturate, put almost all its
weight on one key, and pass back vanishing gradients, exactly the corner-crowding in the triangle above. Fused
attention kernels like FlashAttention compute it in blocks with a *running* max, rescaling the partial sums whenever a
bigger score turns up, so they get the stable version without ever holding a whole row of scores at once.
