A car with a stopped wheel and an engine idling at 850 rpm has a speed mismatch. The engine can't
run any slower than idle, and the wheels can't start at anything faster than zero. The clutch
deals with that mismatch. It's two friction surfaces: the **flywheel** bolted to the engine and a
**disc** splined to the gearbox. Springs clamp them together, and the pedal pulls the springs
off.

While the surfaces slip against each other, the clutch passes torque from the faster side to the
slower one. How much torque depends only on how hard they're clamped, not on how fast they slip.
Once the two speeds meet, the surfaces lock and turn as one shaft.

Drag the pedals. They stay where you leave them, and once one is focused the arrow keys nudge it
(hold Shift for bigger steps). To pull away:

1. Press the clutch to the floor and pick **1**.
2. Lift slowly. Nothing happens over the bottom of the travel because the disc isn't touching yet.
   When the pedal enters the dashed **bite** band, the disc touches. The engine trace dips and the
   idle control opens the throttle a little to hold the revs up.
3. Release the handbrake. On the flat, idle torque alone will creep the car forward.
4. Keep lifting. The shaded gap on the trace is slip, and the two lines close as the car speeds up.
   When they meet, the status reads **locked** and the gas pedal drives the wheels directly.

## Why it stalls

The disc grips as hard as the pedal says, however fast the engine is turning. If you let the pedal
up quickly, the clutch asks the engine for more torque than it can make at idle. The engine
slows towards the wheels' speed instead of the other way round, and it stops once it drops under
about 300 rpm. Try dumping the clutch with no gas. Then do it again with some gas held and watch
the engine trace dip less.

Holding the brakes with the clutch up stalls it too: the wheels are pinned at zero, so the
engine is dragged down to zero with them.

## Hill starts

Set the hill to 12% and try the same launch without the handbrake. The car rolls back as soon as
you come off the brake, because at idle the bite point can't hold the slope. The usual technique:

- With the handbrake on, add some gas (about half) and lift into the bite until the engine
  trace sags and the torque readout climbs past what the slope needs.
- Release the handbrake. The clutch is already holding the car, so it moves forward instead of
  back.
- Keep feeding the clutch up while holding the gas.

Steeper hills need more torque through a slipping clutch for longer. That's the heat you can
smell on a steep street. The red glow in the schematic scales with slip power (torque × speed
difference).

## The bite point moves

Clutch discs wear thinner over time, so the springs have to travel further before they clamp,
and the bite moves higher up the pedal. The **Bite point** slider shifts it. A high bite gives
you less travel above it to feed the clutch in smoothly.
