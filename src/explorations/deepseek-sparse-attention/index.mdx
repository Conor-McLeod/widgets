In ordinary (dense) attention, a token at position <M t="t" /> compares its query with the key of every
token before it, then mixes their values by the softmax of those scores. Each new token therefore
costs work proportional to the length of the context, and a whole sequence costs <M t="O(L^2)" />. At
128K tokens of context that is the dominant expense, both in prefill and in every decoding step.

Yet after the softmax, most of those weights are tiny. A query usually puts its weight on a
handful of keys: some nearby tokens, a few far-back tokens whose content it needs, and a sink at
the start. If you knew in advance which keys mattered, you could skip the rest.

[Sliding-window attention](/sliding-window-attention) guesses "the most recent ones". That's cheap
but blind: anything outside the window is gone, however relevant. **DeepSeek Sparse Attention**
(DSA), introduced in DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp in September 2025, guesses by *content*. A small, fast
scorer called the **lightning indexer** rates every earlier token, and the real attention then
runs over only the top <M t="k = 2048" /> of them.

## Two passes: score cheaply, attend expensively

For query token <M t="t" /> and each earlier token <M t="s" />, the indexer computes

<M block t="I_{t,s} = \sum_{j=1}^{H^I} w^I_{t,j}\,\operatorname{ReLU}\!\left(q^I_{t,j} \cdot k^I_s\right)" />

That is attention-like, but deliberately stripped down. It has a few small heads <M t="j" />, each with
its own query <M t="q^I_{t,j}" /> but one key <M t="k^I_s" /> shared by all of them. A per-head weight <M t="w^I_{t,j}" />,
also computed from the query token, says how much to trust each head. There's no softmax and no
value mixing, just a ReLU, which is cheap and keeps the scores easy to compare. And it runs in FP8.
The indexer's only job is to *rank* keys, not to produce an output.

Then the main attention runs as usual, but only over the selected set:

<M block t="u_t = \operatorname{Attn}\!\left(h_t,\ \{\, c_s \mid I_{t,s} \in \operatorname{Top\text{-}k}(I_{t,:}) \,\}\right)" />

Here <M t="c_s" /> is the cached entry for token <M t="s" />. In DeepSeek-V3.2 that's the
[MLA latent](/mla), and that detail matters: MLA's absorbed form behaves like multi-query attention,
where every head reads the same latent, so one selection per token works for all 128 heads.

Things to try:

- Start on **last k**, a sliding window. It catches the nearby tokens and misses the far-back
  spikes, so it covers only a small share of the weight.
- Switch to **exact top-k**. This is the oracle: it reads the <M t="k" /> heaviest keys, wherever they are.
  It's the best any <M t="k" />-key subset can do, but it needs the dense scores you were trying to avoid.
- Switch to **indexer top-k** and drag **indexer warm-up** to 0. An untrained indexer picks
  almost at random. Raise it and watch the ranking line up with the top panel and the coverage
  climb to the oracle's. The KL readout measures how far the indexer's view (put through a softmax)
  is from the dense attention.
- Lower <M t="k" /> to 3 or 4. Even a good indexer starts to lose weight when the budget is
  smaller than the number of keys that really matter.

## The mask is different for every row

With a sliding window every query reads the same diagonal band. DSA's pattern is decided per query
by content, so each row of the mask is its own scattered set.

Hover or tap a row. In **DSA top-k** mode, notice the cells far left of the diagonal: the sink at
column 0 and the content matches, which a window of the same size can't reach. Compare the
**avg weight covered** between **sliding window** and **DSA top-k** at the same <M t="k" />. Both read the
same number of keys; DSA spends that budget better.

The stats also show the catch. DSA still computes an indexer score for every (query, key) pair, so
the indexer itself is quadratic. The saving comes from it being much cheaper per pair than the
main attention.

## Where the saving comes from

So DSA doesn't remove the <M t="O(L^2)" />. It shrinks its constant and moves it into the cheap indexer.
Per new token, with <M t="L" /> tokens of context, the cost is roughly

<M block t="\underbrace{r \cdot L}_{\text{indexer}} \;+\; \underbrace{\min(L, k)}_{\text{main attention}} \qquad \text{vs.} \qquad \underbrace{L}_{\text{dense}}" />

where <M t="r" /> is the indexer's cost per key as a fraction of the main attention's. A rough count for
DeepSeek-V3.2 puts <M t="r" /> around <M t="1/30" />. The main attention has 128 heads, each scoring a
576-wide key and mixing a 512-wide value. The indexer has 64 heads of width 128, scoring only,
in FP8.

<Widget src="cost" height={420} caption="Attention cost of one new token, as a share of dense attention's cost at 128K. Counts multiply-adds only." />

Move along the chart, then tick **zoom** to see the DSA curve up close. Below <M t="k" /> tokens the two curves coincide, because DSA reads everything and
the indexer is pure overhead. Past <M t="k" /> the main-attention part flattens at <M t="k" /> keys, and the
only thing still growing is the indexer's slope <M t="r" />. Make the indexer more expensive and the
saving shrinks, with <M t="1/r" /> as the ceiling. That's why it uses ReLU, no softmax, few heads and FP8.

Real speedups also depend on memory traffic and kernel efficiency, which this ignores. DeepSeek's
own measurements of V3.2 at 128K show prefill and especially decoding getting several times
cheaper per token than V3.1, with the gap widening as the context grows.

## Teaching the indexer what to pick

The indexer is new, so it starts out knowing nothing; that's the warm-up slider above. DeepSeek
didn't train V3.2 from scratch. They continued from V3.1-Terminus, which already had dense
attention, in two stages:

1. **Dense warm-up.** Keep dense attention and freeze the whole model except the indexer. The
   target is the main attention's distribution for each query, summed over heads and normalised.
   The indexer learns to match it by minimising
   <M t="\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_{t,:} \,\|\, \operatorname{softmax}(I_{t,:})\right)" />. This took only 1000 steps,
   about 2.1B tokens.
2. **Sparse training.** Switch on top-k selection with <M t="k = 2048" /> and train everything. The
   main model learns from the language-modelling loss alone, now attending over only the selected
   keys. The indexer keeps its KL loss, computed only over the keys it selected, and its input is
   detached so its gradients don't flow into the main model. This stage ran for about 944B tokens.

The point of the warm-up is that the model's existing attention already knows what's relevant.
The indexer just has to learn to predict it cheaply before anything relies on its choices. After
the second stage, DeepSeek reported V3.2-Exp roughly matching V3.1-Terminus on benchmarks while
being much cheaper at long context.

## How it compares

- **Sliding window** picks by position. It's free to decide but can't see past the window
  in one layer.
- **Block-sparse** schemes such as DeepSeek's earlier NSA (native sparse attention) pick whole
  blocks of tokens, which is kinder to GPU memory access but coarser.
- **DSA** picks individual tokens by content. The cost is an extra quadratic pass, and that pass
  is cheap enough not to matter much.
